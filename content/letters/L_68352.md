[unavailable] … The book got a good press[^1] but, apart from the favourable opinions of twenty men of letters, due, I believe, to the articles written by you, Mr Wells and Mr Brock, it is <u>un fiasco discreto</u> [^2]. From what Miss Weaver writes the sales came to a standstill[^3] after a fortnight or so. [unavailable]

… to some repertory theatre, [pioneer players](/people/O_THEPIONEER/) [^4] or [Mr Grein](/people/grei62/) [^5]. Produced or not I intend if possible, to publish it before the end of the year – for I hope to finish <u>[Ulysses](/works/W_ULYSSES/)</u> in time for publication in 1918. Of course no publisher will look at it but if [Miss Weaver](/people/weav76/) makes any margin of profit perhaps it can be published in the same way[^6] as the [last book](/works/W_APORTRAIT/).

I confirm [my telegram](/letters/L_71617/) “Please cable [Quinn](/people/quin70/) [^7] my acceptance offer money to be sent you remitting cable expenses”. Perhaps the 10/– did not cover your expenses. If so, please let me know how much I am in your debt.

I am writing again and am now doing Penelope[^8]. I find I have written more than I thought. But as usual it goes slowly. I am glad to hear you are at work on a poem[^9]. I should like to read your farce[^10] that the [Abbey Theatre](/people/O_ABBEYTHEATRE/) would not put on.

As regards your last copy[^11] of [Dubliners](/works/W_DUBLINERS/) if it does not come back I will give you another copy signed.

Excuse the brevity of this letter in reply to your very kind and long one[^12].

Una stretta di mano [^13]

[James Joyce](/people/joyc82/)

30 March 1917

---

[^1]: JJ had been receiving clippings of reviews of [A Portrait of the Artist as a Young Man](/works/W_APORTRAIT/) (published as a book in [New York](/places/G_USA_NEWY/) in December 1916 and in [London](/places/G_GBR_LOND/) in February 1917) courtesy of Harriet Shaw Weaver and B.W. Huebsch (see LI 100-103; LII 389-95). Positive notices had appeared by Pound, “James Joyce: At Last the Novel Appears,” [Egoist](/works/W_THEEGOIST/), vol. 4, no. 2 (February 1917): 21-22; [H.G. Wells](/people/well66/), “James Joyce,” Nation (London), vol. 20 (24 February 1917): 710, 712, and New Republic, vol. 10 (10 March 1917): 158-60; and Arthur Clutton-Brock, “Wild Youth,” Times Literary Supplement, no. 789 (1 March 1917): 103-04. Wells (1866-1946) found [A Portrait](/works/W_APORTRAIT/)’s claim to importance to be “as good as the claim of the last book of Gulliver’s Travels,” even if JJ did betray “a cloacal obsession” (Nation, 710). Clutton-Brock (1868-1924) wrote that JJ “gives us something that is worth having. It is wild youth, as wild as Hamlet’s, and full of wild music” (TLS, 104). Other intelligent appreciations were written by John Quinn, “James Joyce, a New Irish Novelist,” Vanity Fair, vol. 8, no. 3 (May 1917): 49, 128; J.C. Squire (“Solomon Eagle”), “Current Literature,” New Statesman vol. 9, no. 210 (14 April 1917): 40; Francis Hackett, “Green Sickness,” New Republic, vol. 10 (3 March 1917): 138-39; and James Huneker, “James Joyce,” Unicorns (New York: Charles Scribner’s Sons, 1917), 187-94 (reprinted from the New York Sun). Less enthusiastic reviews also appeared, such as the unsigned piece, “A Study in Garbage,” Everyman (23 February 1917): 398, which called [A Portrait](/works/W_APORTRAIT/) “an astonishingly powerful and extraordinary [_sic_] dirty study of the upbringing of a young man by Jesuits, which ends – so far as we have been at all able to unravel the meaning of the impressionist ending – with his insanity.” Other reviews, including anonymous ones, are listed and quoted in Robert H. Deming, ed., James Joyce: The Critical Heritage, Volume 1, 1907-1927 (London: Routledge & Kegan Paul, 1970), and George Monteiro, “Reviews and Notices of James Joyce in the United States, 1916-1920,” James Joyce Quarterly, vol. 52, no. 1 (Fall 2014): 105-128.

[^2]: JJ’s phrase “un fiasco discreto” plays on the Italian term “successo discreto,” or “modest success,” conventionally used to convey tepid, unenthusiastic approbation of a musical or dramatic performance. “Fiasco discreto” might translate, oxymoronically, to a “modest disaster.”

[^3]: Weaver wrote B.W. Huebsch on 14 March 1917 that after the appearance of reviews by H.G. Wells and Arthur Clutton-Brock, sales of the English edition of [A Portrait](/works/W_APORTRAIT/) “began to make a good start,” but that “this week... they have dropped off” (LII 390-91).

[^4]: JJ was eager to have his still unpublished play, [Exiles](/works/W_EXILES/), staged by some company. The Pioneer Players, founded in 1911 by Edith Craig (1869-1947), was a London theater society, known for producing banned plays, dramas about women’s suffrage and social issues, and foreign plays in English. Performances were put on for private, subscribed audiences, to evade the Lord Chamberlain’s licensing strictures. Craig’s mother, the noted actress Ellen Terry (1847-1928), was president of the Players, and [George Bernard Shaw](/people/shaw56/) was an advisor. The Players staged, among other productions, Shaw’s Mrs. Warren’s Profession (1912), Charlotte Perkins Gilman’s Three Women (1912); Reginald Wright Kauffman’s The Daughters of Ishmael (1914); and Magdalen Ponsonby’s Idle Women: A Study in Futility (1914). They did not perform [Exiles](/works/W_EXILES/). It was first performed in German translation (as Verbannte: Schauspiel in drei Akten) in [Munich](/places/G_DEU_MUNI/) in August 1919, after it had been published as a volume in May 1918 in New York and London.

[^5]: J.T. (Jacob Thomas) Grein (1862-1935) came to London from [Holland](/places/G_NLD/) in 1885 and became an influential theatre producer and critic. He was an early promoter of Shaw and [Ibsen](/people/ibse28/) in London theatres, and established the Independent Theatre there, which put on many private-subscription plays, including Ibsen’s Ghosts and Shaw’s Widowers’ Houses. Grein created controversy by staging Oscar Wilde’s Salome in London in 1918, with Maud Allan in the title role. He did not produce [Exiles](/works/W_EXILES/).

[^6]: JJ’s [A Portrait of the Artist as a Young Man](/works/W_APORTRAIT/) was first serialized in Harriet Shaw Weaver’s magazine [The Egoist](/works/W_THEEGOIST/) (1914-1915) and then published as a book, first by B.W. Huebsch in New York in December 1916 and then by Weaver’s Egoist Ltd. in London in February 1917. JJ’s [Ulysses](/works/W_ULYSSES/) would be far from finished in 1918. It was partially serialized in [The Egoist](/works/W_THEEGOIST/) (1919) and in [The Little Review](/works/W_THELITTLE/) (1918-1920), before being issued as a book in February 1922 in [Paris](/places/G_FRA_PARI/) by [Sylvia Beach](/people/beac87/)’s Shakespeare and Company and also, in October 1922, by the Egoist Press of London, assisted by [John Rodker](/people/rodk94/) who dispatched copies to purchasers from Paris (Ellmann, JJ 505 n).

[^7]: John Quinn (1870-1924), a collector of modern art and literary manuscripts, offered JJ £20 for his hand-corrected pages that had been used to set the American edition of [A Portrait](/works/W_APORTRAIT/), published by Huebsch in December 1916. JJ and Quinn transacted the purchase through Pound, whom JJ had cabled this message circa 19 March 1917. For a full account of the purchase, see that [telegram](/letters/L_71617/) and [JJ’s letter to Pound, 19 March 1917](/letters/L_68348/), notes.

[^8]: “Penelope,” the last of the eighteen episodes of [Ulysses](/works/W_ULYSSES/), was initially drafted, along with other late episodes, early in JJ’s work on the book, as he suggested in letters to Harriet Shaw Weaver (LI 143; LII 387; see also Michael Groden, Ulysses in Progress [Princeton, NJ: Princeton University Press, 1977], 77).

[^9]: Pound wrote JJ in his letter of 17 March 1917 that he had “begun an endless poem, of no known category. Phanopoeia or something or other, all about everything.... Probably too sprawling and unmusical to find favour in your ears. Will try to get some melody into it further on” (Pound/Joyce 102). These tentative beginnings of Pound’s “endless poem,” eventually to become his lifelong project, The Cantos, were published in installments as [“Three Cantos,”](/works/W_THREECANTOS/) Poetry, vol. 10, nos. 3-5 (June, July, August 1917): 113-21, 180-88, 248-54. Pound wrote in a note to the first installment: “As Poetry circulates among people definitely interested in the art, I do not feel apologetic about presenting the opening cantos of an exceeding long poem. Most of the long poems that one can read were written before printing was invented, and circulated in fragments. More recent precedent may be found in the publication of separate cantos of Don Juan” (Poetry [June 1917]: 167).

[^10]: During his stay with [W.B. Yeats](/people/yeat65/) (1865-1939) at Stone Cottage in Sussex in the winter of 1913-1914, Pound worked on translations of Japanese Noh plays, drawing on unpublished manuscripts of Ernest Fenollosa (1853–1908), an American scholar of Japanese art. Pound published his translations along with an introduction in “Noh” or Accomplishment: A Study of the Classical Stage of Japan (London: Macmillan and Co., 1916), and, with an introduction by Yeats, in Certain Noble Plays of Japan (Churchtown: Cuala Press, 1916). His work on these short, intense, symbolic plays fascinated Yeats, who found in them a new direction for his own drama and wrote At the Hawk’s Well, an abstract, hieratic, one-act play based on the Irish mythological hero Cuchulain and his quest for immortality. Yeats’s play was first performed, with music and dance, in April 1916 at the London home of Lady Cunard (1872-1948), and was published in 1917.Pound at this time also wrote a number of original short plays inspired by Noh drama. In his letter to JJ of 9 February 1917, he expressed doubt that [the Abbey Theatre](/people/O_ABBEYTHEATRE/) in [Dublin](/places/G_IRL_DUBL/) would stage JJ’s [Exiles](/works/W_EXILES/), despite Yeats’s interest in JJ’s work (Pound was right). He then added: “They gibbed at a one act farce of mine on account of _indecency_” (Pound/Joyce 92 [emphasis in original]). Around September 1916, he similarly wrote Iris Barry: “Yeats made me lengthen a skit of mine before he would take it for the Abbey. (Later rejected by the manager on the grounds that its indecencies would cause a riot in Dublin.)” (Selected Letters of Ezra Pound, 1907-1941, ed. D.D. Paige [New York: New Directions, 1971], 96). Pound’s “skit” or “farce” was most likely The Consolations of Matrimony, which combined Synge-like folk drama, Irish vernacular, and Japanese farce. At Stone Cottage, he had become interested in Synge, and he signed his short play with the pseudonym “Oge Terrence O’Cullough.” The play is an earthy, humorous dialogue of two men about women and marriage, with Syngean lines such as: “Ah you could have cracked a flea on either one of her breasts” (see James Longenbach, Stone Cottage: Pound, Yeats, and Modernism [New York: Oxford University Press, 1988], xi, 209-213). Pound expected that his farce and Yeats’s At the Hawk’s Well would be performed together, perhaps in the manner of a satyr play paired with a noble tragedy. He had written his father in February 1916 that their efforts were towards “a new dramatic movement, plays which won’t need a stage, and which won’t need a thousand people for 150 nights to pay the expenses of production. [Yeats’s] play and a brief skit of mine will be done in Lady Cunard’s big room, in, I suppose, April” (quoted in Longenbach, 205). In the end, Pound’s play was not performed at Lady Cunard’s home. Perhaps this was on the ground of indecency, as with the Abbey’s rejection, or perhaps, as Moody suggests, it was because there wasn’t time to rehearse both plays (A. David Moody, Ezra Pound: Poet, A Portrait of the Man and His Work, vol. 1 [Oxford: Oxford University Press, 2007], 293). The Consolations of Matrimony and other short plays by Pound were published in Plays Modelled on the Noh (1916), ed. Donald C. Gallup (Toledo, OH: Friends of the University of Toledo Libraries, 1987).

[^11]: Pound wrote JJ on 17 March 1917: “I have a new copy of Dubliner’s [_sic_], as your first copy went out propaganding [_sic_] and never came home” (Pound/Joyce 103).

[^12]: That is, Pound’s long letter of 17 March 1917 (Pound/Joyce 100-103).

[^13]: It. a handshake.
