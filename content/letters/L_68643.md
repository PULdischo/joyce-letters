editorialNotes:
- 'This letter, written entirely in French, is a witty, in-kind reply to Pound’s letter
  to JJ of 10 August 1920, which Pound dated “10 Aout,” addressed to “Monsieur,” and
  signed with the phrase “votre temperately devoué E.P.” (Robert Spoo, “Unpublished
  Letters of Ezra Pound to James, Nora, and Stanislaus Joyce,” James Joyce Quarterly,
  vol. 32, no. 3-4 [Spring-Summer 1995]: 537). In part, JJ is mocking his own stiff,
  formal social demeanor in Paris. The English artist and writer Wyndham Lewis (1882-1957),
  who spent time with him in summer 1920, later recalled the “staid, copybook French”
  that he used in conversation and his unbending “_You First... After You_!” manners
  (Blasting and Bombardiering [London: Eyre & Spottiswoode, 1937], 293, 300). JJ was
  defensively aware of his habit in Paris of “addressing people I have just met for
  the first time as ‘Monsieur’” and the reputation he was earning for being “_a tout
  petit bourgeois_” (LI 166; see also [JJ to Pound, 18 August 1920](/letters/L_68645/)).
  Thanks to David Spurr for assistance with the transcription and translation.'

> [rue de l’Assomption 5](/places/G_FRA_PARI_05/),
> Passy
> [Paris](/places/G_FRA_PARI/)

Monsieur: J’ai dûment reçu votre honorée du 10 courant contenant un ~~cheque~~ chèque (£6-0-0) et un ordre de paiement sur [MM Thomas Cook et Fils](/people/O_COOKANDSON/) de cette place et je vous prie d’accepter mes ~~remerciments~~ remerciements [^1] empressés. M’étant rendu au bureau de ladite maison, place de l’Opéra, j’ai présenté le chèque à un charmant jeune homme qui se tenait à l’autre côté d’un guichet. Il me pria très courtoisement de vouloir bien y mettre ma signature et après m’avoir fait mille compliments il s’empressa à me donner le montant en valeur française, à savoir, francs 294 (deux cent quatre ~~ving~~ vingt quatorze). Je lui fis une profonde ~~référence~~ révérence et pris congé de lui en lui souhaitant le bonjour, lui exprimant en même temps ma reconnaissance pour sa bienveillance vraiment exquise.

Je suis désolé de ne pas pouvoir déchiffrer, malgré des efforts répétés, à l’aide d’une loupe, le nom du monsieur qui a eu la ~~gentilesse~~ gentillesse de m’envoyer cette somme. En tout cas, Monsieur, je vous en ~~saurai~~ saurais infiniment gré si vous ~~voudriez~~ vouliez bien avoir ~~l’obligéance~~ l’obligeance de lui transmettre de ma part mes ~~remerciments~~ remerciements très sincères et de lui assurer que je ne manquerai pas à la lui rendre aussitôt que j’aurai reçu l’argent que j’attends d’un jour à l’autre. En effet comme vous aurez ~~peutêtre~~ peut-être entendu, un [monsieur américain](/people/quin70/) très riche et également aimable, a eu la bonté d’acheter le manuscrit[^2] d’un [petit livre](/works/W_ULYSSES/) que j’ai écrit de temps à autre pendant mes moments de loisir. Il voulait en outre me témoigner ~~dans une~~ d’une manière tout à fait spéciale son appréciation de mes pauvres pages et au lieu de me remettre l’argent d’achat moyennant une banque ou un mandat de poste il s’est décidé à faire la traversée de l’océan à la nage pour m’apporter personnellement son don de ~~mécénate~~ mécénat. Il est accompagné ~~par un~~ d’un vieux monsieur, peintre ~~rénommé~~ renommé, dans une embarcation[^3] faite exprès pour l’occasion qui, tout en voguant ~~energiquement~~ énergiquement, l’encourage avec des cris retentissants d’admiration et d’espoir. On signale déjà l’arrivée de ces deux héros aux [îles Açores](/places/G_PRT_AZOR/). Je ne trouve pas de paroles pour exprimer le vif émoi dont cette preuve de générosité me remplit.

Selon vos conseils j’ai télégraphié immédiatement[^4] à [mes avocats](/people/O_SLACKMONRO/) à [Londres](/places/G_GBR_LOND/) qui se sont hâtés à me répondre qu’ils seront enchantés de m’envoyer avec un avion spécial toute somme, n’importe quelle, dont je pourrais avoir ~~besoins~~ besoin. Aussi [mon agent](/people/pink62/), homme vraiment charmant, m’écrit me priant d’accepter deux timbres poste de dix centimes à titre de cadeau[^5]. Inutile ~~à~~ de vous dire, Monsieur, comme je suis profondément touché par ces actions si nobles et ~~désinteressés~~ désintéressés.

Je m’aperçois avec un grand plaisir que vous venez de lancer avec succès éclatant [votre revue[^6] tellement attendue <u>The</u> <u>Die</u> <u>All</u>](/works/W_DIAL/). Ayant appris votre sobre langue dans ma jeunesse j’ai trouvé le titre d’un bon sens et d’une justesse plutôt uniques que rares de nos jours. On pourrait le rendre en français – tous crèvent de faim: mais cela est beaucoup plus expressif dans la langue du divin Scheckspère. C’est très chic, l’anglais. Seulement, Monsieur, je n’ai pas bien compris la première parole: <u>The</u> [^7]. Est-ce que c’est du <u>faive</u> <u>o</u>’<u>klohk</u>?

En ce moment je viens de recevoir une lettre ~~extrèmement~~ extrêmement aimable de [M. Eliot](/people/elio88/). Il m’écrit qu’il me porte un wagon de vêtements usés que vous, Monsieur, ~~a~~ avez eu la bonté de collectionner parmi les charmantes personnes qui ont lu mon tout [petit livre](/works/W_ULYSSES/) afin que je puisse entrer sans difficulté dans le haut monde ici[^8]. C’est vraiment gentil de leur part et je vous prie, Monsieur, de leur communiquer tous mes sentiments de dévouement. Même s’il y aura des ~~pétites~~ petites différences de ~~mésure~~ mesure ou de taille vous pouvez dorénavant assurer ces messieurs et ces dames charitables que je ne ~~hesitérai~~ hésiterai pas à me soumettre à des opérations chirurgiques que les autorités compétentes jugeront ~~necéssaires~~ nécessaires.

En conclusion, Monsieur, permettez-moi de vous renouveler mes ~~remerciments~~ remerciements les plus empressés et avec l’expression de ma profonde reconnaissance je vous prie d’agréer l’assurance de ma parfaite considération[^9].

[James Joyce](/people/joyc82/)

le 13 août 1920

Sir: I duly received your esteemed favor of the 10th instant enclosing a check (£6-0-0) and money order on Messieurs Thomas Cook & Son of this city and I beg you to accept my earnest thanks. Having gone to the office of said firm, place de l’Opéra, I presented the check to a charming young man who stood on the other side of a counter. He asked me very courteously to place my signature on it and after making me many compliments he hastened to give me the sum at the French exchange rate, namely 294 francs (two hundred ninety-four). I made him a deep bow and took my leave wishing him good day, at the same time expressing to him my gratitude for his truly exquisite kindness.

I regret that I am unable to decipher, despite repeated efforts with the aid of a magnifying glass, the name of the gentleman who had the goodness to send me this sum. In any case, Sir, I will be infinitely grateful to you if you would be so obliging as to convey to him my very sincere thanks and to assure him that I will not fail to repay him as soon as I have received the money that I am expecting from day to day. Indeed as you will perhaps have heard, a very rich and equally kind American gentleman was good enough to purchase the manuscript of a little book I wrote now and again in my moments of leisure. He wanted moreover to show me in an altogether special way his appreciation for my humble pages and instead of remitting to me the purchase money by means of a bank or postal money order, he decided to swim across the ocean to bring me his gift of patronage personally. He is accompanied by an elderly gentleman and renowned painter who, in a boat built expressly for the occasion, while rowing vigorously, cheers him on with ringing shouts of admiration and hope. The arrival of these two heroes at the Azore islands has already been reported. I have no words to express the lively emotion with which this display of generosity fills me.

On your advice, I cabled immediately to my solicitors in London who hastened to reply that they will be delighted to send me by a special airplane any sum, no matter what, of which I might have need. Also my agent, a truly charming man, writes begging me to accept two ten-centime postage stamps as a gift. I need not tell you, Sir, how deeply touched I am by such noble and unselfish acts.

I note with great pleasure that you just launched with brilliant success your long-awaited magazine <u>The</u> <u>Die</u> <u>All</u>. Having learned your plainspoken language in my youth I found the title to be sensible and accurate in a fashion more unique than rare in these days. One could render it in French – tous crèvent de faim [all are starving]: but it is much more expressive in the language of the divine Scheckspère. The English is very stylish. Only, Sir, I did not fully understand the first word: <u>The</u>. Is that <u>faive</u> <u>o</u>’<u>klohk</u>?

Just this moment I received an extremely kind letter from Mr. Eliot. He writes that he is bringing me a carriage-load of used clothing which you, Sir, had the goodness to collect from the charming people who have read my very small book in order that I may enter without difficulty into high society here. It is truly kind of them, and I beg you, Sir, to communicate to them my sentiments of devotion. Even if there will be differences of size or cut you can assure these charitable gentlemen and ladies that I will not hesitate to submit myself to such surgical procedures as competent authorities will deem necessary.

In conclusion, Sir, allow me to repeat my most earnest thanks, and with expression of profound gratitude I beg you to accept the assurance of my complete esteem.

[^1]: Pound’s letter to JJ of 10 August 1920 enclosed a “contribution for yr. upkeep and running exp[enses] from deux marchands de tableaux. (£6/ and 26.30 fr)” (Spoo, “Unpublished Letters of Ezra Pound to James, Nora, and Stanislaus Joyce,” 537). It is uncertain who the two art dealers were, and JJ’s present letter states, or pretends, that he cannot read the name – presumably on the check – of the “gentleman who had the goodness to send me this sum.” It is quite possible that Pound was the donor and that the “deux marchands de tableaux” were himself and his wife [Dorothy, née Shakespear](/people/poun86/) (1886-1973), who purchased contemporary art when they could and owned a number of works by [Wyndham Lewis](/people/lewi82/) and other artists. It would have been typical of Pound to sell some of his personal art collection as a way of supporting JJ’s writing. (He had written [Nora Barnacle](/people/barn84/) in September 1917 that he was hoping to augment JJ’s remuneration for the serialized [Ulysses](/works/W_ULYSSES/) by selling autographs of King Ferdinand and Queen Isabella, dated 1492, in his possession [Pound/Joyce 126].) Pound believed in a dynamic flow of financial aid in which patrons would become creative collaborators with worthy living artists in a private gift economy, rather than investing their cash in an impersonal market to acquire the inert products of famous dead or spent artists (on Pound’s concept of patronage, see Robert Spoo, Modernism and the Law [London, Bloomsbury Academic, 2018], 134-36). Pound’s phrase “marchands de tableaux” may echo JJ’s humorous hope for a “merchant of jalap” who would come to his economic rescue (see [JJ to Pound, letter fragment, late July 1920](/letters/L_68628/)).

[^2]: The New York lawyer [John Quinn](/people/quin70/) (1870-1924), a collector of modern art and literary manuscripts, had agreed to purchase JJ’s [Ulysses](/works/W_ULYSSES/) manuscript and had cabled JJ an advance of 3,000 lire or $153.85 on 5 February 1920. On 15 August 1920 he sent another payment of 3,400 francs or $250, and an additional sum of 1,400 francs in the fall of 1920 (Myron Schwartzman, “‘Quinnigan’s Quake!’ John Quinn’s Letters to James Joyce, 1916-1920,” Bulletin of Research in the Humanities, vol. 81, no. 2 [Summer 1978]: 237, 241-42, 245-46, 254, 258). For a fuller account, see [JJ to Pound, 24 July 1917](/letters/L_68393/), notes.

[^3]: JJ’s frustration over Quinn’s slowness to send promised payments takes here the form of a Circean fantasy in which Quinn swims the Atlantic ocean to present JJ with a check, followed in a boat by a cheerleading John Butler Yeats (1839-1922), the noted Irish artist and father of the poet [William Butler Yeats](/people/yeat65/). The elder Yeats, who was a friend of Quinn, once described the latter as “the biggest hearted and most irascible” man in America, as Pound told JJ (Pound/Joyce 110; for a fuller account of J.B. Yeats, see [JJ to Pound, letter fragment, late July 1920](/letters/L_68628/)).

[^4]: JJ was waiting for the London solicitors Monro, Saw & Company to issue his September interest payment from a £5,000 trust in war loan securities, settled on him in 1919 by the magazine editor and patron, Harriet Shaw Weaver (1876-1961). Pound had written JJ in his 10 August letter: “if you want to borrow money, there is no reason why yr. solicitors shdnt now advance you £10/ on yr. Sept. installment. Thus saving me the bloody fuss of gathering same from not particularly willing furze bushes” (Spoo, “Unpublished Letters of Ezra Pound to James, Nora, and Stanislaus Joyce,” 537).

[^5]: JJ was expecting royalties and sums from various sources, and had grown impatient with his London literary agent J.B. Pinker (prob. 1862 or 1863-1922), who was charged with collecting these (LI 143; see also [JJ to Pound, 24 July 1920](/letters/L_68631/), notes).

[^6]: Pound was now a paid foreign agent and correspondent for the New York magazine [The Dial](/works/W_DIAL/). The letterhead for [The Dial](/works/W_DIAL/) – on which Pound typed his 10 August letter to JJ – minimized Pound’s role by simply listing his London address, anonymously, as “Agency,” because the magazine’s wealthy backers, [Scofield Thayer](/people/thay89/) (1889-1982) and James Sibley Watson, Jr. (1894-1982), were concerned that his controversial reputation might create difficulties and were determined to retain editorial control and avoid any “honourable martyrization” for publishing offensive matter (A. David Moody, Ezra Pound: Poet, A Portrait of the Man and his Work, vol. 1 [Oxford: Oxford University Press, 2007], 390-91). Along with Pound, T.S. Eliot, Wyndham Lewis, and some thirty other writers, “James Joyce” was listed on the letterhead as having “provided either acceptable manuscript or promises of collaboration.” JJ’s sole contribution was his poem, “A Memory of the Players in a Mirror at Midnight,” printed in vol. 69, no. 1 (July 1920): 26, and later included in his volume, Pomes Penyeach (1927). In the present letter, JJ imagines himself as a Frenchman trying to puzzle out the magazine’s name phonetically by calling it “The Die All,” an appropriate title for a vehicle that, as JJ bitterly supposed, would be more successful in starving than supporting its contributors.

[^7]: JJ’s imaginary Frenchman wonders if the English word “The” means “tea” (“le thé” in French). In Britain and Ireland, the meal called “tea” often begins at 5 pm (“faive o’klohk”) and can include substantial food as well as beverage. This reference to tea is juxtaposed with the suggestion of starvation (“tous crèvent de faim”).

[^8]: JJ had just received a letter from the American poet and critic T.S. Eliot (1888-1965), dated 11 August 1920, informing him that Eliot would be in Paris briefly on 15-16 August and hoping that JJ could dine with him. He also had a “package” for JJ, courtesy of Pound, that he wished to deliver (The Letters of T.S. Eliot, vol. 1, ed. Valerie Eliot [New York” Harcourt Brace Jovanovich, 1988], 402). JJ did dine with Eliot. Wyndham Lewis, who joined them, later told the story of the parcel: After they had cut the string tied with “the crafty housewifely knots of the cunning old Ezra,” the package opened to reveal, along with some clothes, “a fairly presentable pair of _old brown shoes_” (Blasting and Bombardiering, 275 [emphasis in original]). Lewis believed that Pound’s well-meant charity accounted for JJ’s insistence on paying for all meals and drinks during his and Eliot’s visit. The possibility of receiving gifts of cast-off clothes from Pound was a running joke for JJ as far back as the exchanges leading up to their meeting in Sirmione in June 1920, including JJ’s limerick, written a few days before that meeting, in which he mentioned “boots, clothes [and] money” among Pound’s sacrifices for JJ (see [JJ letter to Pound, 5 June 1920](/letters/L_68611/), notes).

[^9]: Our translation gives a fairly literal rendering of the concluding phrase, but it is in fact a French formula typical of business letters, used by JJ with French correspondents at this time. As Ellmann notes, “Perfect consideration [‘parfaite considération’] is more often accorded to a notary than to a patron” (Ellmann, JJ 487).
