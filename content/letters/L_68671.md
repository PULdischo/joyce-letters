> [rue de l’Assomption 5](/places/G_FRA_PARI_05/),
> Passy
> [Paris](/places/G_FRA_PARI/).

Dear [Pound](/people/poun85/): About a week ago, a remittance of $200[^1] arrived from [Mr Quinn](/people/quin70/) – I presume in reply to some persuasive missive of yours. I have been actively engaged since then buying different articles in shops and interpreting the wishes and counterwishes of sellers and buyers. Will you please give me the name and address of the person who sent the £6[^2] – if that was the amount?

I also received a letter from [Miss Weaver](/people/weav76/) saying that she is going to make me a further donation[^3]. It is not quite clear till I receive her next letter but she says it brings in something like £100 a year. I fancy that your long arm is here also in the background.

[Mr Huebsch](/people/hueb76/) has crossed the herring pond and has reached and left [London](/places/G_GBR_LOND/) without writing either to me or to [my agent](/people/pink62/) or sending anything[^4]. He called on [Miss Weaver](/people/weav76/) and seemed very reluctant to have anything to do with <u>[Ulysses](/works/W_ULYSSES/)</u>. She says that the printer who was reading <u>Ulysses</u> now refuses to print it[^5] and that there is no use offering it to anyone in England.

I never heard anything more of [Mr Fels](/people/fels91/) [^6] but I hope he is in good health. I have also heard nothing either from the <u>[Mercure](/works/W_MERCURE/)</u> [^7] or from [Mr Poe](/people/lugn69/) [^8].

O, Mr Poe, You’re very slow! Et [Monsieur Vallette <!-- alt reading: "Valette" -->](/people/vall58/) [^9] Il nous faut la galette [^10]! So haste to ease us For the love of Jesus! Kreuzbomben, Sakrament [^11]!

My case of books[^12] appears to have fallen into a bed of thistles somewhere in Italy. I have had telegrams sent to [Trieste](/places/G_ITA_TRIE/) about it.

I should be glad to see any new number of the <u>[Little Review](/works/W_THELITTLE/)</u> or <u>[Dial](/works/W_DIAL/)</u> [^13]. [Mr Eliot <!-- alt reading: "Elliott" -->](/people/elio88/) and [Mr Lewis](/people/lewi82/) [^14] were here. The former, on his return, seemed to me rather tired and I felt that I was intruding on his fatigue. But it was the incorrigible [<u>ami</u> <u>Fritz</u>](/people/vand76/) [^15] who howled at me out of a taxicab to be there. Subsequently he bawled at me during dinner because I expressed admiration for [Mr Gide](/people/gide69/) [^16] who, to judge by his last book [<u>La</u> <u>Symphonie</u> <u>Pastorale</u>](/works/W_LASYMPHONIE/), short as it is, seems to know a pen from a pitchfork. Unfortunately I was obliged to take leave towards midnight as my metro train would not wait for the end of the harangue while [Mr Eliot <!-- alt reading: "Elliott" -->](/people/elio88/) and [Mr Lewis](/people/lewi82/) listened with bewilderment while that double Dutchman[^17] bawled after me: Savez-vous ce que vous avez dit?[^18] Vous avez dit une connerie! Et savez-vous ce qui est votre [Monsieur Gide](/people/gide69/)? C’est un con! Et savez-vous ce qu’il écrit, ce con-là? Il écrit de la merde! Foutre! Le con!

These things being thus I have now written the <u>Circe</u> episode in draft but it will need a great deal more work. The movements of recalcitrant sceneshifters[^19] behind the stage have necessitated a number of extra rehearsals.

I hope [Mr Lewis’s](/people/lewi82/) hand and knee are now better. He got a very bad fall[^20] from a bicycle. I think they had not a very pleasant holiday.

sincerely yours

[James Joyce](/people/joyc82/)

P.S. I have taken the liberty of introducing to you a friend of mine [Mr Francis Budgen](/people/budg82/) [^21], a painter, who has gone on to [London](/places/G_GBR_LOND/) from [Zurich](/places/G_CHE_ZURI/).

[^1]: JJ had been waiting for the New York lawyer John Quinn (1870-1924), a collector of modern art and literary manuscripts, to send another payment towards his purchase of the [Ulysses](/works/W_ULYSSES/) manuscript. On 5 February 1920 Quinn had cabled JJ an advance of 3,000 lire or $153.85. On 15 August 1920 he sent another 3,400 francs or $250 (JJ says $200 here), with a further 1,400 francs in the fall of 1920 (Myron Schwartzman, “‘Quinnigan’s Quake!’ John Quinn’s Letters to James Joyce, 1916-1920,” Bulletin of Research in the Humanities, vol. 81, no. 2 [Summer 1978]: 237, 241-42, 245-46, 254, 258). JJ wrote Quinn on 3 September 1920 that the 15 August payment had arrived (LI 145). There was some confusion at this time because a portion of the manuscript sent by JJ from Trieste in June 1920 had not yet reached Quinn (see LI 145; LIII 21-22). Quinn acknowledged receipt of the missing portion, however, on 27 September 1920; at that point he had “received altogether fourteen episodes of ‘Ulysses,’ beginning with ‘Telemachus’ and ending with ‘Oxen of the Sun’” (Schwartzman 253). For additional details, see [JJ to Pound, 24 July 1917](/letters/L_68393/), notes.

[^2]: JJ had received a “mysterious £5 cheque” from Pound in early August 1920 (JJ to Pound, 8 August 1920); and on 10 August, Pound sent JJ a further “contribution for yr. upkeep and running exp[enses] from deux marchands de tableaux. (£6/ and 26.30 fr)” (Robert Spoo, “Unpublished Letters of Ezra Pound to James, Nora, and Stanislaus Joyce,” James Joyce Quarterly, vol. 32, no. 3-4 [Spring-Summer 1995]: 537). It is uncertain who the two art dealers were. It is quite possible that Pound was the donor and that the “deux marchands de tableaux” were himself and his wife Dorothy, née Shakespear (1886-1973). For a fuller account, see [JJ to Pound, 13 August 1920](/letters/L_68643/), notes.

[^3]: JJ learned in May 1919 that an anonymous donor who had gifted him £200 in 1917 had decided to settle on him £5,000 in war loan securities as a trust, the interest to be paid at five percent as a modest but independent income (Mary T. Reynolds, “Joyce and Miss Weaver,” James Joyce Quarterly, vol. 19, no. 4 [Summer 1982]: 380-81). Harriet Shaw Weaver (1876-1961) revealed herself as the donor in a letter to JJ dated 6 July 1919 (John Firth, “Harriet Weaver’s Letters to James Joyce 1915-1920,” Studies in Bibliography, vol. 20 [1967]: 181-82). In late August 1920, Weaver informed him that she was increasing that original gift by another £2,000 (LIII 17). Mary Reynolds describes later increases: “in 1921 another £1,500; in 1923, when [Weaver] received an inheritance, yet another gift of £12,000; and in 1924 a final transfer of £550. (Joyce in a letter to Sylvia Beach in 1923 put the ‘total sum made over to me by her’ at £21,000 …)” (384). For further details, see [JJ to Pound, 24 July 1920](/letters/L_68631/), notes.

[^4]: JJ was hoping to receive royalties due on books from his American publisher, B.W. Huebsch (1876-1964), and also a possible advance on royalties for the book version of [Ulysses](/works/W_ULYSSES/) (for a fuller account of JJ’s need for royalties at this time, see [JJ to Pound, 24 July 1920](/letters/L_68631/), notes). Huebsch came to Paris and met with JJ there in late September or early October 1920 (see LI 148; JJ to Pound, 5 November 1920, notes).

[^5]: Harriet Shaw Weaver had written JJ in late August 1920 that no English printer would take the risk of setting up [Ulysses](/works/W_ULYSSES/) as a book (LIII 17). Other plans were being considered at this time. Huebsch had hoped to issue the book in the United States, and was still considering the possibility, but he was discouraged by the anti-obscenity movement in America and the fact that several numbers of [The Little Review](/works/W_THELITTLE/) containing portions of [Ulysses](/works/W_ULYSSES/) had been seized by the U.S. post office. JJ began to worry that “Huebsch is crying off [Ulysses](/works/W_ULYSSES/)” (LI 144; LIII 17). The New York lawyer John Quinn was urging JJ to consider a private edition, to be published by Huebsch if possible, limited to 1,000 or 1,250 copies selling for $8 or $10 each. He thought that, later on, a trade edition might be published “from the same plates if at that time Huebsch wants to run the risk of prosecution.” No fan of immoral writing, Quinn grumbled: “The present orgy of disgusting sex novels may be over by that time and the public opinion may sway the other way so that a trade edition of Ulysses might go without prosecution.” Quinn thought that a private edition of [Ulysses](/works/W_ULYSSES/) might escape prosecution as had George Moore’s A Story-Teller’s Holiday, published in 1918 through Boni and Liveright of New York in a limited edition “privately printed for subscribers only,” selling for $10 a copy (Quinn to JJ, 15 August 1920, John Quinn Memorial Collection, Manuscripts & Archives Division, New York Public Library). Also, John Rodker (1894-1955), English writer and poet, was hoping to be the publisher of [Ulysses](/works/W_ULYSSES/) in some book format, but this plan did not come to fruition. Instead, [Ulysses](/works/W_ULYSSES/) was first issued as a book in February 1922 in Paris by [Sylvia Beach](/people/beac87/)’s [Shakespeare and Company](/people/O_SHAKESPEAREANDCOMPANY/), and then in October 1922 by the Egoist Press of London, assisted by Rodker who rented a room in Paris from which he dispatched copies – printed in France to avoid the squeamishness of English printers – to purchasers in America, Britain, and elsewhere (Ellmann, JJ 505-06 n.†). The edition’s title page states: “Published For The | Egoist Press, London | By John Rodker, Paris | 1922.”

[^6]: Florent Fels (1891–1977), French publisher, journalist, and author, had planned to publish a French translation of [A Portrait of the Artist as a Young Man](/works/W_APORTRAIT/) in his journal, Action: Cahiers de philosophie et d’art (LI 120; The Cornell Joyce Collection, 69-70). Candidates for translator were, first, Louis Bagnan (LII 450), then Ludmila Bloch-Savitzky (LI 142), but Fels produced only a review of the novel in Action, no. 6 (December 1920): 63–64. The translation by Savitzky (1881-1957) was published as a book, Dedalus; Portrait de l’artiste jeune par lui-même, by the Paris house, Éditions de La Sirène, in 1924.

[^7]: JJ was also hoping that a French translation of [A Portrait of the Artist as a Young Man](/works/W_APORTRAIT/) might be published serially in the journal Mercure de France, founded and edited by Alfred Vallette (1858-1935). But on 20 October 1920, JJ reported to Jenny Serruys that the Mercure had given “aucune signe de vie” (“no sign of life”) (LIII 25). Other French journals declined it as well (for a fuller account, see [JJ to Pound, 18 August 1920](/letters/L_68645/), notes).

[^8]: Aurélien-Marie Lugné (1869-1940), known as Lugné-Poë, was a French actor and theater director who was considering a Paris production of JJ’s play [Exiles](/works/W_EXILES/). In June 1921, however, he concluded that staging [Exiles](/works/W_EXILES/) would lose money (Ellmann, JJ 497-98). For a fuller account, see [JJ to Pound, 18 August 1920](/letters/L_68645/), notes.

[^9]: For Alfred Vallette, editor of the Mercure de France, see note 7 above; [JJ to Pound, 18 August 1920](/letters/L_68645/), notes.

[^10]: Fr. literally, “We need the pastry cake (or biscuit)!” A popular sense of the word “la galette” is “money” or “cash” (Nouveau Petit Larousse [Paris: Librairie Larousse, 1972], 458). Hence, JJ’s meaning here is “We need the dough (money)!”

[^11]: Ger. literally, “Cross bombs sacrament!” These curses, popular since the nineteenth century, took various forms such as “Kreuz – Bomben! – Donnerwetter!” or were euphemized as “Kreuz – Bomben – Element!” (see, e.g., Frank Wedekind, Franziska: Ein Modernes Mysterium [1912], in Ausgewählte Werke, vol. 5 [Berlin, 1923], 50).

[^12]: JJ sent a case of books and papers from Trieste on 29 June 1920 for his use in Paris (“to enable me to write”), and when it was delayed, he began to suspect that the sender – Società Adriatica di Spedizione – was doing “what they could to upset me and any plans I had” (LIII 21, 23). The “whoreson Adriatica” finally delivered the case to JJ on the evening of 27 October 1920 (LIII 26). JJ wrote Pound that it arrived “after four months of its odyssey” (LIII 27).

[^13]: JJ’s Ulysses had been appearing serially in the New York magazine [The Little Review](/works/W_THELITTLE/) since March 1918. What he did not yet know was that, as he was writing this letter, the New York Society for the Suppression of Vice was preparing to file an official complaint against the July-August 1920 number, which contained the portion of the “Nausicaa” episode in which Leopold Bloom masturbates at the seashore (see Paul Vanderham, James Joyce and Censorship: The Trials of Ulysses [London: Macmillan, 1998], 41-42). JJ’s sole contribution to the New York magazine [The Dial](/works/W_DIAL/) was his poem, “A Memory of the Players in a Mirror at Midnight,” printed in vol. 69, no. 1 (July 1920): 26, and later included in his volume, Pomes Penyeach (1927).

[^14]: The American poet and critic T.S. Eliot (1888-1965) visited Paris on 15-16 August and dined there with JJ and the English writer and artist Wyndham Lewis (1882-1957). Eliot and Lewis then traveled together in France – the Breton coast, Nantes, Saumur, and other places – but the trip was disrupted after Lewis injured his hand in a bicycle mishap. Eliot saw JJ again, at least twice, when he was in Paris with Lewis in later August (The Letters of T.S. Eliot, vol. 1, ed. Valerie Eliot [New York: Harcourt Brace Jovanovich, 1988], 402-09). For a fuller account of Eliot and Lewis’s initial encounter with JJ in Paris, see [JJ to Pound, 13 August 1920](/letters/L_68643/), notes; [18 August 1920](/letters/L_68645/), notes.

[^15]: Fritz-René Vanderpyl (1876-1965), Belgian (often described as Dutch) novelist, poet, gastronome, and art critic for Le Petit Parisien. Though he was a bit overwhelmed by Vanderpyl’s boisterous conversation and personality, JJ liked him and spent time with him in Paris (for a fuller account, see [JJ to Pound, 18 August 1920](/letters/L_68645/)).

[^16]: André Gide (1869-1951), French author and Nobel Prize winner, published his novella [La Symphonie pastorale](/works/W_LASYMPHONIE/) in La Nouvelle Revue Française in 1919. It is a tale of physical, emotional, and moral blindness and the love of an older man (who is a pastor) and his son for the same sightless girl, named Gertrude. JJ read it in Zurich, and also liked Gide’s novel Les Caves du Vatican (1914). Later, Gide privately called [Ulysses](/works/W_ULYSSES/) “a sham masterpiece,” and opposed including the French translation Ulysse in the Éditions de la Pléiade (Ellmann, JJ 487-88, 530).

[^17]: “Double Dutch”: “Gibberish, jargon, of a foreign tongue not understood by the hearer” (E. Cobham Brewer, Dictionary of Phrase and Fable, new ed. [London: Cassell, 1923], 358). “Double Dutchman” often meant someone speaking nonsense or falsehood. “‘Never mind,’ was Mac’s philosophic remark; ‘if we don’t come here again I’m a double Dutchman’” (E.R. Harrison, “With Mac to Hadrian’s Wall,” The Boy’s Own Paper, vol. 30, whole no. 1542 [1 August 1908]: 699). Or it could signify a confused thinker or writer: “‘Sir, he is a double Dutchman, floundering in a continent of mud’” (“John Owen,” Cyclopædia of English Literature, ed. Robert Chambers, vol. 1 [Boston: Gould, Kendall & Lincoln, 1850], 458). Since at least the time of English trade and martial conflict with Holland, “Dutch” could be a term of abuse or derision. In [Ulysses](/works/W_ULYSSES/), Leopold Bloom thinks of alcoholic drink as inspiring “Dutch courage” ([U](/works/W_ULYSSES/) 8.1043).

[^18]: Fr. “Do you know what you said? You said a bloody stupid thing! And do you know what your Mr. Gide is? He’s a bloody idiot! And do you know what that bloody idiot writes? He writes shit! Dammit! The bloody idiot!” (This could be rendered more colorfully, and vulgarly, since “con” can be translated as “cunt,” “asshole,” or “prick,” and “Foutre!” is a strong expletive.) JJ admired certain works of the French author André Gide (1869-1951), especially his novella [La Symphonie pastorale](/works/W_LASYMPHONIE/) (1919), which he praised in the presence of Vanderpyl, eliciting the outburst he records here (for more details concerning JJ and Vanderpyl, see [JJ to Pound, 28 September 1920](/letters/L_68674/), notes).

[^19]: JJ was working at this time on “Circe,” the fifteenth and longest episode of [Ulysses](/works/W_ULYSSES/). It is written in the form of a drama, with dialogue and stage directions. JJ announced to Pound in a Christmas card dated 20 December 1920 that “Circe” was “finished this morning at last” (LIII 34). (For a fuller account of “Circe,” see [JJ letter to Pound, 5 June 1920](/letters/L_68611/), notes.) The “sceneshifters” who were necessitating “extra rehearsals” (multiple revisions) could be anyone interfering with JJ’s concentration, from his noisy family, to the Blochs who were forcing him to shift his scene (that is, find a new flat), to persons in Paris and abroad who weren’t carrying out his wishes quickly enough.

[^20]: During their holiday together in France in August 1920, T.S. Eliot (1888-1965) and Wyndham Lewis (1882-1957) rented bicycles for getting about. The handlebars fell off Lewis’s machine, throwing him to the ground and causing him to injure his hand. He worried that he had contracted lockjaw from the wound (Peter Ackroyd, T.S. Eliot: A Life [New York: Simon & Schuster, 1984], 102; for more details, see [JJ to Pound, 28 September 1920](/letters/L_68674/), notes).

[^21]: The English painter Frank Spencer Curtis Budgen (1882-1971), with whom JJ formed a close personal and literary friendship (for a fuller account, see [JJ to Pound, 1 September 1920](/letters/L_68671/), notes).
